# One-week stock-picking principles

## Scope

These rules are for long-only trades in liquid U.S. stocks held for about five trading days. The goal is to pursue profit from a short-lived catalyst, not to predict which stock will produce the absolute highest return. No process can guarantee that. Risk controls stay in the process because one uncontrolled loss can erase several profitable trades.

## 1. Trade only stocks I can enter and exit efficiently

I will consider only NYSE- or Nasdaq-listed common stocks priced at least $5, with market capitalization of at least $2 billion, median daily dollar volume of at least $20 million, and a normal bid/ask spread no wider than 0.5%. I will prefer limit orders instead of paying any available market price.

**Why I can defend it:** A five-day edge can be small, so spread, slippage, and market impact matter. Research on post-earnings drift found that apparent profits were concentrated in illiquid stocks and estimated that transaction costs consumed 70–100% of the paper profits. The SEC explains that limit orders control the worst acceptable price, although they do not guarantee execution.

## 2. Require a fresh, checkable catalyst—not a rumor

The candidate must have a material event within the last two trading days. My preferred catalyst is an earnings report in which results beat the published consensus and management maintains or raises forward guidance. I will verify the event using the company's earnings release and SEC filing, then confirm that at least one independent reputable source reports the same facts.

**Why I can defend it:** A catalyst gives the one-week trade a reason to exist. Academic evidence describes post-earnings-announcement drift: prices can continue moving in the direction of an earnings surprise for several weeks. I am using the first week of that possible drift, after the information is public, rather than gambling before the report.

## 3. Make price and volume confirm that buyers agree

I will enter only if the stock closes above its pre-catalyst closing price, stays above its 20-day moving average, and catalyst-day volume is at least 1.5 times its 20-day median. I will not buy if the stock has already gained more than 15% from the prior close; that is a chase, not a controlled entry. When several stocks qualify, I will rank them by catalyst quality first, then five-day relative strength versus the S&P 500, then liquidity.

**Why I can defend it:** The news alone is not enough; the market's reaction is a second piece of evidence. Positive price and unusually high volume show that investors treated the announcement as meaningful. The 15% ceiling keeps me from confusing a dramatic spike with a repeatable one-week setup.

## 4. Define the exit before buying

Before entry I will record the entry price, the catalyst-day low, the five-day deadline, and the maximum dollar loss. I will risk no more than 1% of the trading account on one idea, never average down, and exit at the first of: five trading days, the thesis becoming false, a daily close below the catalyst-day low, or a gain equal to twice the planned loss. A broker stop is an alert and execution tool, not a guarantee of the final sale price.

**Why I can defend it:** The thesis is specifically about a one-week reaction, so a time exit prevents a failed trade from quietly becoming a long-term holding. The two-to-one payoff means the process does not need to win every time. FINRA warns that a stop becomes a market order and can execute far from the stop price during volatility, so position size—not the stop alone—limits damage.

## Rejected principle: buy the day's biggest percentage gainer

I rejected the rule “buy whichever stock rose the most today and hold it for a week.” A concrete failure case is a $2 biotech that gaps 80% on early clinical-trial headlines: it fails my price, size, and liquidity filters; it can be halted; the spread can widen; and a later financing can dilute shareholders. Most importantly, the earnings-drift research found that illiquid names showed the largest paper anomaly while trading costs consumed 70–100% of those apparent profits. The spectacular chart is exactly where an executable edge may disappear.

## Five-minute checklist

1. Is it an eligible, liquid NYSE/Nasdaq common stock?
2. Is there a public catalyst from the last two trading days, verified in a company release or SEC filing?
3. Did price and volume confirm the catalyst without a greater-than-15% chase?
4. Have I written the entry, invalidation, position size, two-to-one profit level, and five-day exit?
5. If any answer is no, do not call it a pick.
